‘Handsome, boyish – and he can cook!’ Richard O’Sullivan landed on TV like a lovable alien

It was taken as read in the 1970s that all women, given the chance, would go out with him. But the star wasn’t just a new man – he was a radical presenceRichard O’Sullivan, who has died at the age of 82, arrived in acting by accident. As a boy, he’d come back from a family holiday in Ireland with a strong accent, and his parents sent him to the Corona Theatre School in London to soften it. This origin story was very much in keeping with his adult self-fashioning: blokey, lovable, down to earth, cheeky – not the kind of guy who would go to drama school for the purposes of drama. But his work as a child star was almost back to back. He filmed his first feature aged eight, as an extra in The Yellow Balloon, and the next role he landed was the lead in The Stranger’s Hand, released the following year (1954). He played Audrey Hepburn’s younger brother in The Nun’s Story in 1959, and Elizabeth Taylor’s in Cleopatra four years later. It’s famously not that easy, making the child-to-adult actor transition, and the higher your profile as a junior the harder it is. It was while straddling the awkward between-years that O’Sullivan appeared in two Cliff Richard flicks, The Young Ones and Wonderful Life in the early 60s, and his facility with that pastel-shaded wholesomeness fed into but only partly described the comic actor he would become.His first lead role as an adult was opposite Beryl Reid in Alcock and Gander, in 1972. Creators Johnnie Mortimer and Brian Cooke found their muse in O’Sullivan. Man About the House aired the following year, with O’Sullivan playing Robin Tripp, a trainee chef. The setup sounds so unremarkable now that it’s hard to fathom it as a break from any norm: two women (played by Paula Wilcox and Sally Thomsett) are looking for a flatmate. It’s assumed it’ll be another woman who’s all ready to move in; instead, O’Sullivan appears in their bath after a party. It was almost an ET archetype – this alien arrives, this man who can cook, and spices up all their lives, to great merriment – and they can only get him past the landlord by pretending he’s gay.
