‘In our lands the rain is made of bullets’: Palestinian artists look back in anger, melancholy and defiance

IMMA, Dublin From massacre locations painted on to basalt slabs to photocollages of bulldozed airports, artists from across the Levant address their traumatic historyIn 2006, Simone Fattal painted on to basalt slabs the names of places indelibly associated with massacres in the Levant since 1948: Deir Yassin; Jenin; Qana; Sabra. Through the names, in the same Arabic script, runs a line of poetry by Etel Adnan: “In our lands of drought the rain forever is made of bullets.”Fattal’s work, which documents atrocities on a material that burst from the earth as lava before settling into stone, introduces a show that likewise combines the recording of history with anger both hot and cold. The last named of those massacres, perpetrated by a Lebanese Christian phalangist militia during Beirut’s occupation by Israeli forces in 1982, also inspired the poem Praise for the High Shadow by Mahmoud Darwish, a line from which this show takes as its title. Writing in exile in Beirut, Darwish riffed on the sea as a symbol of Palestinian displacement and memory, connecting people dispersed around the eastern Mediterranean to each other and to their homeland. The angry, melancholy, defiant work of these (mostly) Palestinian artists also weaves a traumatic national history into the cosmopolitan culture of the Levant and the interlinked world in which we now live. Trance, prog synths, guttural screaming: Lorenzo Senni’s All Ages is the year’s wildest electronic album

(Warp)The Milan producer revisits his youth as a straight-edge punk fan voyeuristically dabbling in rave – and finds a thrilling interzone between both genresThe opening line of the biography on Lorenzo Senni’s Bandcamp page is striking. “Lorenzo Senni was never a raver,” it reads, something of a blunt admission from a dance producer, or at least a producer who works in an area of electronica influenced by dance music: one might reasonably assume you need some first-hand experience of the dancefloor’s ineffable freedom in order to make music for it. But the Milan-based Senni has made a USP out of his arm’s length distance from what that Bandcamp bio calls a “neon-hued chemical world”.A straight-edge punk fan in the late 90s, he accompanied friends to Rimini’s hardcore and gabber clubs as a kind of anthropological exercise, a self-styled “rave voyeur” who “just liked to observe and analyse my surroundings”. Accordingly, his albums to date have dealt in what approving critics like to call deconstructions or interrogations of dance music rather than dance music itself: 2012’s Quantum Jelly and 2014’s Superimpositions featured what Senni called “pointillistic trance”, spinning the icy, staccato notes and arpeggiated synth lines of the genre’s epic breakdowns into lengthy, beatless tracks that never reached the climax implied. Released at the height of the 2020 Covid lockdown – when everyone was, by default, some distance from the dancefloor – his last album, Scacco Matto, expanded his palette to include lush ambient electronics, quasi-classical chord structures and something of the teeming chaos of Rustie’s digital maximalism. The paintings are square, because a square is neutral, and he didn’t want viewers associating them with the visual language of landscape or portrait. For Ryman, his paintings resolutely were what they were, which is to say, white paint applied to various surfaces and fixed to the wall. Not “windows” through which one could glimpse another world.At the National Gallery, a couple of miles across town, is an exhibition of 10 works by the 15th-century Flemish painter Jan van Eyck. With one exception – the Arnolfini portrait, which is a one-off in all kinds of ways – they are all A4-size portraits, each angled so that we see more of the left than the right side of their faces. Aside from the likenesses of the people who paid for them in some of his religious paintings, including the Ghent altarpiece, these are the sum total of Van Eyck’s surviving portraits.
