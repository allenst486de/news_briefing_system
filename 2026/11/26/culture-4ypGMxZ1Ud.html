Anselm Kiefer review – Holocaust-haunted forests and a glimpse into a gigantic grave
The Guardian 2026-11-26 02:37 ⭐ 주요 뉴스

White Cube Bermondsey, LondonKiefer asks what art means after the Holocaust – but even the vast scale of his powerfully seductive paintings can’t do justice to the gravity of his themesWhen the world is falling apart, the artist retreats to the studio. It’s an old story, and Anselm Kiefer has always loved the old stories, drawing for his monumental and reliably blockbuster paintings on narrative traditions ranging from Norse mythology to German Romantic poetry. So it makes sense that an artist who has dedicated his life to the question of what art means after the end of European civilisation – which is to say, after the Holocaust – should now seek out the solitude of the studio to make sense of what’s coming. Kiefer’s exhibition at the same gallery a decade ago had the title Walhalla, after the beer hall in the sky in which dead Vikings drink away the afterlife. Now comes the more modest (you would think) subject of the artist’s studio, depicted here in a suite of lustrous landscapes and a simple wooden cabin in the forest. Rendered in flat gold leaf, the sky in these paintings gleams like a tarnished halo in an old altarpiece, while the verdigris grass pushes roughly off the canvas. Here is the place where heaven meets the earth and is transmuted – by the alchemy of this painter’s touch – into quietly numinous paintings containing elements of both.If you can put aside the fact that Kiefer’s own studio is a vast warehouse complex on the outskirts of Paris, these paintings present a very German vision of communion with nature. They also evoke the most famous hut in modern philosophy: the Black Forest hideaway where Martin Heidegger formulated his argument that “authentic” culture must be rooted in the native landscape. The flipside of this idea, that nature is not just a scenic backdrop to human history but a living participant in it, would have, let’s just say, consequences for German painting after 1945. Ten years later, and after an almost continuous decade of Trumpism, DuVernay has made a follow-up film about the 14th Amendment, and she has evidently arrived at a rather different attitude to the constitution and its authors’ intentions and good faith.
